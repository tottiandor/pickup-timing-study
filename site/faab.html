What a bid buys

You are paying
for a fight

Three quarters of waiver claims are made for nothing at all — on priority, at $0. Of the ones that do cost money, the expensive end does worse on every measure that matters, including the one you would think money controls: whether you win the claim.

Win rate climbs, peaks, then collapses

Up to about $9 the money works as you would expect. Above that it goes into reverse. Not because a higher bid loses an auction — it cannot, the top bid wins — but because a big bid is a signal that you are in a fight, and most people in a fight lose it. You bid $25 on the player everybody else is chasing too.

And it buys nothing on the pitch

The dearest claims start least often and score least per week they are held. The only thing that rises with the price is how long the manager keeps him — and that is a sunk cost, not a verdict on the player.

In defence of the big bid

One honest objection to everything above: an expensive claim is often a player who will not kick a ball for weeks — an injury return, a new signing, somebody about to get a run. Judging that on the gameweek after the claim says nothing about whether it was a good buy. The data agrees.

Claims that never started at all for that team

the real dud rate

Over a third of free claims never played a minute for the team that took them. Above $25 it is one in ten. Money buys a near-certainty that he eventually turns up — after an average wait of — gameweeks, against almost none for a free claim.

But not a better player

Points per start is the one measure with tenure stripped out. Total points while held rewards simply owning somebody a long time, however badly he played — and expensive claims are held far longer, so that measure flatters them. PPS asks only: when he did start, what did he score?

Points per start, by what you paid

tenure removed

It runs the wrong way. Free claims returned — per start against — for $10+ — a gap of 1.03 ± 0.22, which is 4.8 standard errors and real. The money buys dependability of minutes, not quality.

Which does not mean never spend. FAAB is use-it-or-lose-it, and teams got through $89 each on average, so unspent budget is worth nothing. In absolute terms a $10+ claim returned 72.8 points while held against 20.7 for a free one. Per dollar that is poor; in points it is not. The defensible advice is not "don't spend" — it is don't spend early, where you pay roughly fifteen times the price for the same quality of footballer.

Two thirds of contested auctions were won without spending a penny. Grouping properly by division — the only place two claims actually compete — the median winning margin over the best losing bid was $0.00, and 7% of auctions were won by someone who bid less than the loser. Waiver priority is deciding a great many of these, not money.

What eighteen divisions know

Each division is a separate market with its own managers. Counting how many of them independently chased the same player in the same gameweek is a real consensus signal — and the crowd turns out to be worth listening to, up to a point.
